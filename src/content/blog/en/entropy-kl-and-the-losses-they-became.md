I type `CrossEntropyLoss` more often than I think about it. That is what a good abstraction is for. But the decision behind the name is still there, and when a model trains strangely, the loss is usually where the strange thing lives.

The useful way to read a probabilistic loss is as a description length. Every such loss quotes a price for encoding data under some distribution. Once I hold that frame, entropy, cross-entropy, and KL stop being three formulas and become three sentences in one argument.

## Entropy is a bill, not a mood

For a discrete distribution $p$,

$$
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[-\log p(x)\right].
$$

The term $-\log p(x)$ is the surprise of one outcome, and it is also the number of nats an optimal code needs to transmit it. Entropy is the average bill. MacKay builds a whole book on this: compression and inference are one problem seen from two sides, and a log-probability is a length.

Two consequences matter. The base of the logarithm is a unit, not a definition, so nats and bits differ by a constant. And entropy is a property of a distribution rather than a sample, so the value printed in a training loop is a noisy estimate over a batch.

## Cross-entropy is the price of the wrong code

If the data comes from $p$ and I encode it with a model $q$, the average length is

$$
H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q).
$$

Cross-entropy is never below $H(p)$, and the difference is the KL. That identity explains why the loss works at all: $H(p)$ is fixed by the data, so training can only move the wasted bits. For one-hot labels $H(p) = 0$ and cross-entropy collapses to negative log-likelihood, which is why it is easy to mistake for a plain distance to the label.

## KL is the extra cost, and it is not a distance

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
$$

Gibbs' inequality gives non-negativity, with equality if and only if $p=q$ almost everywhere. That is the end of the metric-like properties. It is not symmetric, it does not satisfy the triangle inequality, and it is only defined when $p$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $q$: if $p(x) > 0$ and $q(x) = 0$ anywhere, the value is infinite.

The infinite case is the mechanism, not a technicality. A model that assigns zero probability to an event that occurs receives an unbounded penalty, which is why implementations add an epsilon or work in log space — and why a hard zero in a softmax is a bug rather than a confident prediction.

Geometrically, KL is the Bregman divergence generated by negative entropy. That gives it a projection structure and a Pythagorean identity inside exponential families. It still is not a metric, but it explains why maximum likelihood has clean asymptotics.

## Maximum likelihood is a KL projection

For the empirical distribution $\hat p$ of a sample,

$$
-\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log q_\theta(x_i) = H(\hat p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat p\,\|\,q_\theta).
$$

The entropy term does not depend on $\theta$, so maximising likelihood is minimising the forward KL to the empirical distribution. Maximum likelihood is therefore one direction of one divergence, estimated from one sample, rather than a separate principle that happens to work.

## The framing breaks here: KL has a direction, and the direction decides the fit

This is the part where the standard framing misleads. A function called `kl_loss(p, q)` implies a symmetric quantity. The divergence has a direction, and the two directions fit different things.

Forward KL, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$, averages over $p$. Where $p$ has mass and $q$ has almost none, the log ratio explodes, so $q$ is pushed to cover everything $p$ covers. This is mass-covering, also called zero-avoiding. Reverse KL, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$, averages over $q$: regions where $p$ is near zero are penalised heavily, so $q$ would rather sit inside one mode than spread across several. This is mode-seeking, or zero-forcing.

Both are correct answers to different questions. In a variational autoencoder the term is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))$ — an expectation under the encoder, which pushes the encoder's mass inside the prior's support. In distillation the usual objective is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{teacher}}\|p_{\text{student}})$ — an expectation under the teacher, so the student is asked to cover the teacher. Swap the arguments and the student imitates the teacher's argmax and ignores the rest of the soft labels.

A numeric reminder of the asymmetry:

```python
import numpy as np
p, q = np.array([0.9, 0.1]), np.array([0.5, 0.5])
kl = lambda a, b: float(np.sum(a * np.log(a / b)))
print(kl(p, q), kl(q, p))   # 0.368 0.511
```

Same two distributions, two numbers. Any report of "the KL" without a named direction reports half a quantity.

The support issue has teeth in preference optimisation. The KL penalty to a reference policy is estimated by sampling from the policy being trained, so it measures the reverse direction relative to the reference. That direction permits the policy to concentrate on behaviour the reference also allows. When I want coverage instead, I need the other direction or a divergence with a bounded ratio.

## Changing the target on purpose

Two common techniques are, in this language, edits to $\hat p$. Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target with $1-\epsilon$ on the true class and $\epsilon/(K-1)$ elsewhere. Cross-entropy against that target has a non-zero floor, so the optimiser cannot drive the logit gap to infinity — the model is told that the label is not certain information. Distillation replaces the target with the teacher's soft distribution at some temperature, which carries similarity between classes rather than only the decision.

Both are best read as changing what the data means, not as regularisation bolted on afterwards.

## What I check now

When a loss misbehaves I check four things: which direction the KL runs, whether the target has zero mass anywhere the model can put mass, what the reduction does across the batch, and whether the reported number is per token or per sequence. Perplexity is $\exp$ of cross-entropy in nats, and a per-token average hides a long tail of easy tokens. Any of those four can make a healthy run look broken.

Entropy gives the floor. Cross-entropy gives the payment. KL gives the part of the payment that is the model's fault. Keeping the three apart has been more useful to me than any particular loss function.
